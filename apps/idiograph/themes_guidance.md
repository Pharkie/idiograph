# Themes guidance

The lines the PETs screen shows in the board panel under Guidance, the way the Experiential
statements screen shows its rail. Distilled from `pet_guidance.md` beside this file, which is the full guidance the AI
proposal, the title suggestion and the review read, and that file from Smith, Flowers and Larkin
(2022), Smith and Nizza (2022), Nigbur and Chatfield (2025) and the project's supervision notes.
Each heading is a small group in the panel and each line under it starts with `- `. The server reads
the file afresh on every open, so edit it here and it changes in the app on the next reload. Keep
every line short enough to take in at a glance, and the whole short enough to read in a minute.

## How many

- Three to five PETs tell the three to five most important things this case says. Six only with a reason.
- A PET is a pattern, so it holds more than one statement. A lone statement joins its nearest PET, widens a title to hold it, or drops.
- A finished case placed about two thirds of its statements. Not everything connects to everything.

## How to group

- Scatter first. Every statement counts the same, the last as much as the first.
- Gather what belongs together, by what the experience is, what it does, when it comes or how it contradicts itself, never by shared words. One statement may already name what the others share, and lend the title.
- Contrasts belong together. An experience at odds with itself is one PET, left unresolved.
- Where time carries the meaning, let a PET follow the sequence.
- Sub-themes only for distinct facets, past five or so statements, each with two or more. A PET with one sub-theme has none.

## Titles

- A grounded interpretative summary. Five to nine words as a norm, up to about twenty when a shorter title loses the meaning.
- An experience with its stance, never a topic, a category or a question the interview asked. "Dreading the first day back, then finding colleagues kind", not "Work challenges".
- Plain words at the participant's temperature, mixed feelings left mixed. No jargon, no hyperbole, no cause.
- A title tells a small story: a movement or turn in the participant's experience, from one thing to another, a cost or a reversal, not a static label.
- A title has existential merit when it names what the experience was like, with the participant's stance. The merit lives in the experience itself and needs nothing added. A bare event or a narrative fact fails.
- A title packs a punch. It is vivid and memorable, carries the participant's own charged word where there is one, and lands on first reading.
- A broad word that erases what distinguishes the statements fails the PET. The participant's own phrase can serve when it holds the whole.

## Group, stack or bin

- Most statements go into a PET. Group a statement with the ones it belongs with.
- Stack a statement under a better one when it says the same point again, one point said several ways close together in the transcript. The top stands for the stack in the table and the exports.
- Bin a statement only for a reason: it does not answer the research question, it is badly evidenced, or it is irrelevant in some way. Binning is rare, so record why.
- Awkward, negative, unresolved or contradictory experience is no reason to bin. It can hold a PET of its own.
- Watch for your hopes for the topic shaping the grouping.

## Inside a PET

- The statements that carry the PET best go first. Higher on the canvas is the better example.
- One or two quotes carry each PET, picked for richness, feeling, metaphor and range. Check the title against them.
- A better quote can sit under another statement. Map every statement to its quote before settling.

## Before you finish

- Does every statement fit the title, and does the title reach every statement?
- Do two PETs share a core? Then they may be one PET with two facets.
- Does a title say only what happened, or what it was like for the participant?
- Does a title tell a small story, have existential merit, keep the stance whole, and land on first reading?
- Does any title claim a cause, or speak in your theory rather than their words?
