# Themes guidance

The lines the Themes board of a thematic analysis project shows in the board panel under Guidance, the way
the Coding screen shows its rail. Written from Braun and Clarke's reflexive thematic analysis (2006, 2019,
2021, 2022), phases 3 to 5: generating initial themes, developing and reviewing them, and refining,
defining and naming them. Each heading is a small group in the panel and each line under it starts with
`- `. The server reads the file afresh on every open, so edit it here and it changes in the app on the
next reload. Keep every line short enough to take in at a glance, and the whole short enough to read in a
minute.

## What a theme is

- A theme is a pattern of shared meaning, held together by one central organising concept. Name that concept and the theme is clear.
- A theme is not a topic summary, everything participants said about one thing. "Walks as the one hour that belongs to me" is a theme, "Experiences of walking the dog" a topic.
- A question the interview asked is not a theme.
- You build themes from your codes. They do not emerge from the data or wait in it to be found.
- Themes come late, out of the coding. Decided before coding, they would be a codebook, a different method.

## Generating themes

- Gather codes that share a core idea into candidate themes. A rich code can grow into a theme, and a theme can become a subtheme.
- Candidate themes are provisional. Try several groupings and let go of the ones that do not hold.
- How often a code recurs is not how much it matters. A theme earns its place by what it says about your research question.
- A code that fits no theme waits in Not placed. Not every code ends in a theme.

## Developing and reviewing

- Read each theme's extracts together. Do they cohere around its central concept?
- Then go back to the whole dataset. Does the set of themes tell the story of the data for your question, and has the rereading turned up more?
- Each theme needs clear edges. Two that share a core may be one theme. One that holds two ideas may be two.
- A theme with too little to say, or too much, may split, merge or go. Losing a theme is part of the work.
- Watch for a theme that is a heap of codes with no idea holding them, and for themes you hoped to find.

## Defining and naming

- Write each theme's definition in its description: its central concept, what it covers and what it does not.
- If you cannot say what a theme is about in a sentence or two, it needs more work.
- A name is short and informative, and tells the reader what the theme is about. One word is rarely enough.
- A participant's own phrase can make a good name when it holds the whole theme.
- Each theme tells its own story, and together they tell the story of the data, related but not repeating each other.

## The thematic map

- The board is your thematic map. Its layout shows how the themes sit beside and under one another.
- Use subthemes sparingly, for a facet of a theme that needs its own space. Too many thin the analysis.
- An overarching theme can gather themes that share an idea under them.

## Before you finish

- Can you name each theme's central organising concept in a sentence?
- Is any theme a topic summary, or an interview question in other words?
- Do two themes share a core?
- Does every extract under a theme fit its definition, and does the definition reach every extract?
- Do the themes, read in order, answer your research question?
- Where did your own perspective shape the themes, and is that in your notes?
