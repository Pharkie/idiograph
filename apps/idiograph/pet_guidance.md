# Personal experiential themes: guidance for grouping and review

Sections 1 and 3 feed the suggested titles for a PET, and section 4 those for a GET. The GETs screen's Guidance tab shows sections 4 and 5. The AI offers titles, and every analytic judgement stays with the analyst.

## 1. What a personal experiential theme is

A personal experiential theme (PET) is a cluster of one participant's experiential statements under a title that expresses what they share (Smith et al., 2022, pp. 93–95, and Smith & Nizza, 2022, p. 46). It is personal because it belongs to one case, experiential because it concerns the participant's experience or sense-making, and a theme because it holds across the account, not at one place in it. It is not a topic, a category, a question from the interview schedule, a cause, or a tally.

## 2. How statements become themes

1. Scatter, then gather. Break transcript order and treat every statement as equal, the last in a list as much as the first (Smith et al., 2022, pp. 90–92, 98–99).
2. Abstraction. Statements that belong together go under a new name that says what they share (Smith et al., 2022, p. 98). The book says like with like, and the likeness is one of meaning, stance or function, never of the words the statements happen to use, and it is one device among several, not the rule.
3. Subsumption. One statement already names what the others share and lends the cluster its title (Smith et al., 2009, ch. 5).
4. Stacking. Near-identical statements go one on another, the top one speaking for the stack (Smith et al., 2022, p. 97).
5. Polarization. Contrasting statements sit together to show an experience at odds with itself (Smith et al., 2022, p. 98).
6. Narrative, close to the first edition's contextualization. When time carries the meaning, a cluster follows a sequence, a phase or a life course (Smith et al., 2022, p. 98).
7. Function. A cluster gathers what the talk does, a way of making sense or presenting oneself, titled with doing and being words (Smith et al., 2022, p. 98).
8. Numeration. Recurrence is one signal of weight, never its measure (Smith et al., 2009, ch. 5).
9. Clustering the clusters. Small clusters join into larger ones and stay as sub-themes, or a large cluster divides (Smith et al., 2022, p. 97).

## 3. Rules for a theme

1. Three to five themes for a case, six only with a reason. The table tells the three to five most important things the case says (Smith & Nizza, 2022, ch. 4). A finished case in this project placed about two thirds of its statements in five themes, one of them with sub-themes.
2. A theme holds more than one statement, since a theme is a pattern. Fold a lone statement into its nearest theme, widen a title to hold it, or let it drop (Smith & Nizza, 2022, ch. 4).
3. Each statement sits in one theme, so the table stays an evidence trail from title to timecode (Smith et al., 2022, pp. 95–97).
4. Discard only what does not answer the research question. What goes depends on the question and its scope (Smith et al., 2022, p. 90), and a statement unrelated to the others and doing no analytic work drops (Smith & Nizza, 2022, ch. 4). Awkward or negative material is no reason. Record why each goes.
5. The title is a grounded interpretative summary, five to nine words as a norm and up to about twenty when a shorter title loses the meaning (project rules). It expresses the convergence of its statements (Smith & Nizza, 2022, p. 46) and names an experience with its stance, never a category (Nigbur & Chatfield, 2025). A PET can be the experience itself or the participant's sense-making of it (Smith et al., 2022, Box 5.5, p. 94), so a title that names what the experience was like, with the stance, needs no cause or consequence added. A bare event, a narrative fact or a description without the stance fails. The participant's comparisons and evaluations are their own reflection and stand in their own terms (Smith et al., 2022, Box 8.1, pp. 135–136). Compare the invented "Work challenges" with "Dreading the first day back, then finding colleagues kind". A title also does three things (project rules). It tells a small story, a movement or turn in the participant's experience, from one thing to another, a cost or a reversal, not a static label. It has existential merit, which lives in the experience itself: a title that names what the experience was like, with the participant's stance, has it. And it packs a punch: it is vivid and memorable, carries the participant's own charged word where one exists, and lands with a reader on first reading, at the temperature of its statements. The title holds what the theme holds and never shrinks rich material. A question the interview asked is not a theme.
6. Register. Plain words at the participant's temperature, sentence case, no therapeutic jargon, no hyperbole, no causal claim within or between themes (project rules). Ground a metaphor or drop it, since a reified metaphor floats free of its statements. A broad word that erases what distinguishes its statements fails the theme. A participant's own phrase can serve when it captures the whole theme (Nigbur & Chatfield, 2025).
7. Sub-themes only where a theme holds distinct facets, as a rule past five or so statements (Smith & Nizza, 2022, ch. 4). Each needs two or more statements, and a theme with one sub-theme has none. A sub-theme can be a variant, an exception, a case in point or a context. Overlapping sub-themes may be one phenomenon.
8. Negative, unresolved and contradictory experience is as valid as the welcome kind and can hold a theme of its own (project rules). Keep contradictions together, unresolved (Smith et al., 2022, p. 98). Watch for the analyst's hopes for the topic shaping the grouping. Ambivalence stays beside gladness, never smoothed into it.
9. Inside a theme, the statements that carry it best go first. Chronology or taxonomy serves where it shows the theme better (Smith & Nizza, 2022, ch. 4).
10. One or two quotes carry each theme, chosen from its statements' key quotes for richness, feeling, metaphor and range (Smith et al., 2022, p. 114). Check the title against them, and that quote and statement share a time frame. Map every statement to its quote before settling, since a better quote can sit elsewhere.
11. Recurrence grounds a theme: a pattern found many times cannot be called invented. Three to five rich statements can hold a theme (Smith & Nizza, 2022, ch. 4), and not everything connects to everything.

## 4. Group experiential themes

A group experiential theme (GET) is a pattern of shared and distinctive features across the participants' PETs, built by looking across every case's PET table for where the cases converge and where they diverge (Smith et al., 2022, ch. 5, Step 7). It holds the participants together and keeps each one apart. It is never a group norm, an average or a summary of the sample.

1. Convergence and divergence. A GET says what its participants share and how each lives it differently, and the divergence is as much a finding as the convergence (Smith et al., 2022, ch. 5, Step 7).
2. Each in their own way. A GET shows in each participant's own way, and every item under it keeps its participant, sheet and timecode, so it traces back to every case it rests on (Smith et al., 2022, ch. 5, Step 7).
3. Moving between levels. A PET is a starting point, never a box. A GET can draw on a PET or on one of its sub-themes, so a sub-theme can sit under one GET and its PET under another. A sub-theme or a statement from one case can make a GET of its own, and material from several cases can make a GET no PET named (Smith et al., 2022, ch. 5, Step 7).
4. Every participant checked. Check every participant for every GET, and say where a case has nothing under it, since a pattern found in a few cases is not a pattern of the group (project rules).
5. Recurrence is one signal of a GET's weight, never its measure. His minimum sets how many participants a GET needs, starting from Smith's band of every participant for three or fewer, three for four to eight and half beyond eight (Smith, 2011). An important GET can rest on a smaller pattern, a broad GET gathers more instances than a narrow one, and the decision is made openly (Smith et al., 2022, ch. 5, Step 7).
6. Negative, unresolved and incomplete trajectories are as valid as the rest and can hold a GET of their own. A participant whose experience runs against the others belongs under the GET as its divergence, never left out to keep it tidy (project rules).
7. The title is his, developed through his interpretation and never said to have emerged. It is a grounded interpretative summary close to the participants' own words, typically five to nine words, sentence case, with no therapeutic jargon and no hyperbole (project rules, and Nigbur & Chatfield, 2025). It names what the experience was like for the participants, with their stance, and so has existential merit, which lives in the experience itself. It also tells a small story of the group's experience, a movement or turn, and packs a punch, vivid and memorable, in the participants' own charged word where one exists (project rules).
8. No cause. A GET says what the experience is like and what it comes to mean, never why it happened, within a GET or between GETs. A participant's own link between things is reported as theirs (project rules).
9. Sub-themes only where a GET holds distinct facets, as in a PET (section 3, rule 7).

## 5. Questions a reviewer asks of a GETs board

1. Does this GET say what its participants share, and does it also show where they part?
2. Has this GET become a group norm or an average, with the particular participants lost in it?
3. Does every participant under this GET show in their own way, or does one case carry it for the rest?
4. Has every participant been checked for this GET, and is a case with nothing under it said?
5. This GET sits under the minimum: does its material show its weight, and is the decision to keep it made openly?
6. Does this GET rest on how often something recurs, when the material in each case is thin?
7. Is a sub-theme or a statement doing work that a GET of its own might hold, or does material from several cases make a GET no PET named?
8. Are the negative, unresolved or incomplete trajectories here, or have they been left in Not placed?
9. These two GETs share a core: are they one GET with two facets?
10. Does this title speak in the participants' language, or in the researcher's theory, jargon or metaphor?
11. Does this title claim a cause or an explanation?
12. This PET is left out of every GET: does the group need it, perhaps as the divergence of one?
13. Does this title tell a small story of the group's experience, a movement or turn, or is it a static label?
14. Does this title have existential merit, naming what the experience was like for the participants with their stance, or does it name only an event, a fact or a topic?
15. Does this title land on first reading, in the participants' own charged word where there is one, or is it flat and forgettable?
